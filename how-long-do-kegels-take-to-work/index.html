How long do kegels take to work?

By Kyrylo Lozovyi · August 10, 2026

Short answer: Expect nothing for the first three to four weeks. The trials that found benefit measured at three and six months, and the men who improved trained daily throughout. Realistic milestones: better awareness and control by week 4, noticeable ejaculatory control around weeks 8–12, erectile changes between 3 and 6 months. If twelve weeks of consistent, correct practice has produced nothing at all, the problem is technique or the cause isn't muscular — and either way, more reps is the wrong response.

This is the most common question about pelvic floor training and the one the marketing answers worst. "Results in two weeks" is a sales claim. Here is what the research timeline actually looks like, and what you should expect to notice at each point.

The honest timeline

WhenWhat changes
Week 1–2 You learn to find and isolate the muscle. Nothing else. If you feel a difference in function this early, it is expectation rather than tissue.
Week 3–4 Control improves: cleaner contraction, longer hold without recruiting glutes or abdominals, more complete release. This is genuine progress and is not an outcome.
Week 6–8 First functional changes for some men — usually ejaculatory control, occasionally less post-void dribbling. Muscle adaptation is real by now.
Week 8–12 The window in which most responders in the premature ejaculation study were improving. Erectile changes may begin.
3 months The first fair assessment point. In the Dorey trial the training group was significantly ahead of controls here (p < 0.001).
6 months The full effect: 40% of men back to normal erectile function, 35.5% improved, 24.5% unchanged.

Why it is slow

Two reasons, and neither is avoidable.

The first is ordinary muscle physiology. Early strength gains in any muscle are largely neural — you get better at recruiting what you already have — and only later does the tissue itself change. The pelvic floor is no exception, and it starts from a worse place than most muscles because almost nobody has ever trained it deliberately.

The second is that the outcome you care about is downstream of the muscle. Better contraction has to translate into better venous compression, which has to translate into better-maintained rigidity, which has to happen often enough for you to notice a pattern. Each of those steps adds lag.

What to do at each checkpoint

Week 4: check technique, not results

Nothing should have changed functionally, so judging outcomes now tells you nothing. Judge the contraction instead. Use the mirror test: a correct contraction visibly retracts the base of the penis and lifts the testicles, with the stomach, buttocks and thighs completely still. If you cannot see that, the next eight weeks will not work, and fixing it now is the highest return available. The technique checks →

Week 12: the real decision point

Twelve weeks of consistent, correct daily practice with no change at all is meaningful information. It usually means one of:

  • The technique was wrong all along — the commonest answer, and the reason the clinical trial used a physiotherapist and a biofeedback device rather than a leaflet.
  • The cause isn't muscular. Vascular, hormonal, medication-related or psychological ED does not respond to this, no matter how well you do it. What else has evidence →
  • The pelvic floor was too tight, not too weak. If twelve weeks of training has made pain, urgency or hesitancy worse, stop. That is a different problem with an opposite treatment →

What it does not mean is that you should do more repetitions. This is a small muscle that fatigues quickly, and an over-trained pelvic floor generates its own problems.

After 6 months: maintenance

Detraining is real, so the gains need some ongoing input — but not the full daily programme forever. Dropping to a few sets a week holds what you built, in the same way that no lifter keeps adding weight indefinitely.

What "consistent" means

The trials trained daily. Not "most days when I remember" — daily, for months, which is exactly why an app exists for this at all. The exercise is free and takes three minutes; the hard part is doing it four hundred times without a reason to.

Common questions

How long before kegels improve erections?

Three months before you can fairly judge it, six for the full effect seen in the research. In the Dorey trial, assessment at three months already showed the training group significantly ahead of controls, and the headline outcome — 40% back to normal function — came from the blinded six-month assessment. Nobody in that trial got there in a fortnight.

How long do kegels take to help premature ejaculation?

The pelvic floor rehabilitation study in men with lifelong premature ejaculation ran twelve weeks, and most of the men who responded were improving by around week eight. Average time to ejaculation went from about 32 seconds to about 146 seconds over the full twelve weeks.

Why am I not seeing results from kegels?

Three usual reasons, in order. Technique — squeezing the buttocks and abdomen instead of the pelvic floor is extremely common and produces nothing. Consistency — a few sets a week is not the intervention the trials tested. And cause — if your erectile difficulty is vascular, hormonal, medication-related or anxiety-driven, a muscle exercise will not address it however well you do it.

Can you feel kegels working?

You feel control before you see results. Within a couple of weeks most men notice they can isolate the contraction more precisely, hold it longer without other muscles joining in, and release it more completely. That is real progress and it is not yet an outcome — it is the prerequisite for one.

Do you have to do kegels forever?

To keep the benefit, largely yes — it behaves like any other muscle training, and detraining is real. The practical version is that the daily programme gets you the change and a substantially lighter maintenance dose holds it, in the same way that nobody keeps adding weight to a lift forever.

Is it too late to start if I'm over 60?

No. The best-known trial had a median participant age of 59 and a range up to 78, so older men were well represented among those who improved. Age changes the odds, not the eligibility.
