Cycling and erectile dysfunction

By Kyrylo Lozovyi · August 10, 2026

Short answer: Mostly no. A large multinational study comparing cyclists with swimmers and runners found no worse sexual function in the cyclists — and the cardiovascular benefit of riding works in the opposite direction, since aerobic exercise is the best-evidenced non-drug intervention for erectile function there is. What does track with trouble is genital numbness: a marker of perineal compression, associated with worse scores, and largely fixable with saddle and position changes. Numbness is the signal. Riding, by itself, is not.

This is one of those health claims that circulates permanently and is rarely checked against the research. The short version: the risk is real, narrower than the folklore, and mostly about pressure rather than about cycling.

What the large studies found

Awad and colleagues surveyed a large, multinational sample of athletes and compared cyclists with swimmers and runners on sexual and urinary function. Nearly 4,000 responses. The cyclists did not have worse sexual function than the other athletes.

That is a cross-sectional survey rather than a trial, with all the usual caveats about self-selection and self-report. But it is a large sample with a sensible comparison group — other endurance athletes rather than the general population — and it is the best answer available to the plain question.

The companion finding is the more useful one. In a related large study, bicycle-related genital numbness was associated with worse scores on the Sexual Health Inventory for Men. Not riding. Numbness.

The mechanism, and why the distinction matters

The perineum — the area between the sit bones — carries the pudendal nerve and the internal pudendal artery, which between them supply sensation and blood to the penis. A saddle that puts your weight there instead of on your sit bones compresses both. Sustained compression reduces blood flow and irritates the nerve, and the immediate result is the numbness cyclists know well.

So the causal chain runs through pressure, not through pedalling. Two riders doing identical mileage can have completely different perineal loads depending on saddle, fit and posture, which is exactly why "does cycling cause ED" gets a muddy answer at the population level while the numbness question gets a clear one.

The other half of the ledger

Cycling is aerobic exercise, and aerobic exercise is the single best-evidenced non-drug intervention for erectile function. The systematic review evidence points to roughly 160 minutes a week of moderate-to-vigorous activity improving erectile function in men whose difficulty is vascular or metabolic — which is most men whose difficulty is physical at all.

What to actually change

  1. Treat numbness as a stop signal. Not something to push through, not something that toughens up. If you go numb, the setup is wrong.
  2. Get your sit bones measured. Most bike shops do it in two minutes. A saddle narrower than your sit-bone spacing forces load onto soft tissue no matter what shape it is.
  3. Consider a cut-out or noseless saddle. Both reduce measured perineal pressure. Neither is magic, and fit still dominates.
  4. Level the saddle. A nose-up tilt drives pressure straight into the perineum. Level, or very slightly nose-down, is the usual fix.
  5. Check the bar height. An aggressive low position rotates the pelvis forward and shifts you off the sit bones. This is why the problem is commoner on road bikes in a racing position than on upright bikes.
  6. Stand up regularly. Out of the saddle for ten to twenty seconds every ten minutes or so restores flow and costs nothing.
  7. Break up very long rides. Risk concentrates in duration and cumulative pressure, not in whether you ride at all.

The pelvic floor angle

Long hours in the saddle are also one of the contributors to a chronically tight pelvic floor, via sustained pressure and the hip flexor shortening that comes with a seated, forward-rotated position. If you ride a lot and have perineal pain, urinary hesitancy or pain after ejaculation — rather than simple numbness during rides — that is a different picture and strengthening exercises will make it worse. Common questions

Does cycling cause erectile dysfunction?

The largest comparison available found no significant difference in sexual function between cyclists and other athletes such as swimmers and runners. The concern is not baseless — the perineum takes load on a saddle, and pudendal nerve and arterial compression are real mechanisms — but at typical recreational volumes the population-level effect has not shown up. Very long durations, high saddle pressure and poor fit are where risk concentrates.

Why do I go numb on the bike?

Because your weight is on the perineum instead of your sit bones. The pudendal nerve and internal pudendal artery run through that area, and sustained pressure compresses both. Numbness is the nerve telling you the pressure is too high for too long — it is the most reliable warning sign in this whole topic and should not be ridden through.

Is genital numbness while cycling dangerous?

It is a signal to act on rather than an emergency. Numbness during riding is associated with worse Sexual Health Inventory for Men scores, and it usually resolves when the pressure does. Persistent numbness that lasts well after riding is a reason to change your setup now and to see a doctor if it does not settle.

What saddle stops numbness?

Whichever one puts your weight on the sit bones rather than the soft tissue between them. Cut-outs and noseless designs both reduce measured perineal pressure, but fit is individual and saddle width relative to your sit-bone spacing matters more than the shape marketing. Get measured at a bike shop rather than guessing.

Should I stop cycling if I have ED?

Almost certainly not. Aerobic exercise has the strongest evidence of any non-drug intervention for erectile function, so stopping is likely to make things worse overall. Change the saddle, the fit and the habits first, and reassess.

How long does cycling numbness take to go away?

Usually minutes to hours after you get off, once the compression stops. Numbness that persists for days, or that appears when you are not riding, is a different matter and worth a medical opinion.
